[Question: "What are your main takeaways this week with regard to cash management and forecasting? Is there anything that is a significant change from the practices at your company currently?"]
My biggest takeaway is that the 13-week and the 13-month forecasts answer two different questions. The 13-week asks "can we meet our obligations?" and the fix is timing: collect faster, pay on terms, defer spend. The 13-month asks "what can we afford to do next?" and the fix is a decision about the pace of expansion, not a timing trick. Until this session I had one annual model and treated it as both.

The significant change is the weekly rhythm. We are pre-opening, so our practice has been to model cash monthly and look at it when something comes up. The session made clear that a forecast nobody rolls every week goes stale. You should never be surprised by your cash balance. We have now set a standing rule: every Monday morning, before anything else, the 13-week forecast is rolled forward and reconciled to the bank.

The second change is about our own business model. Customers pay a refundable deposit on our 20L jar. That deposit is cash in our bank but it is not ours. A large part of our future closing cash will be customers' money, repayable when the jars come back. We now track "cash genuinely ours" separately from the bank balance and manage to that line.

[Question: "Which areas of your business require internal controls? What are the main risks that are relevant to your company?"]
Maji Safi is a centralised water purification plant in Kampala selling 20L refills and single-use water to shops, institutions and households. We open in October 2026. The areas that need controls are:

- Cash and payments. Today one person can initiate, approve, pay and record any payment. Risk: unauthorised, duplicate or unrecorded payments.
- Customer jar deposits. Customers pay a refundable deposit per jar. It is cash in our bank that is not ours. Risk: deposits get spent as if they were profit, then cannot be refunded when jars come back.
- Payroll. We are making our first hires. Risk: pay agreed informally, by email, with no reconciliation or independent approval.
- Tax. VAT, excise and digital stamps are about 29% of gross billings and are collected for URA, plus PAYE and NSSF. Risk: penalties, and spending tax money.
- Information we send outside the company. Funders, lenders and partners receive our figures. Risk: inconsistent or unapproved numbers going out. This has already happened.
- Systems and documents. Risk: the wrong version of a key document gets used or sent, and access is not managed.

[Question: "What types of internal control procedures do you have in place?"]
Few, and mostly designed rather than operating, because we have not started trading.

- A cash-mismatch block in our internal operating system: a day cannot be closed if cash does not match.
- A single source of truth for financial figures, with an approval rule: no figure leaves the company unless finance has marked it approved. Our automated agents must check it before quoting a number.
- Daily automated briefs that flag exceptions: stale files, conflicting figures and overdue items. This works as a detective control.
- A written price book with a B2B discount floor that must be earned on last month's actual volume, never on a promise, and a rule that deposits are never discounted.
- Role-based company mailboxes and an access register listing who should see which financial view.

[Question: "Are there any areas where internal control procedures are missing? Are there any existing control procedures that are not fully implemented? Why are these important - what are the possible consequences?"]
